Current research

Physical AI Foundational Models

Gait laboratories provide detailed measurements of movement, but their equipment and staffing limit routine use during recovery. We are studying how foundational models can learn from movement data, video, and wearable sensors to assess function after hip and knee surgery. The aim is to test whether a model can retain useful accuracy across patients, devices, and clinical settings, and whether its measurements can help clinicians identify recovery that warrants closer assessment.

Biomechanics and Simulation

Surgeons choose fixation, repair configuration, and implant position without directly measuring how those constructs will behave under load. Our patient-specific models examine load sharing, stress concentration, and possible failure in tendon repairs. Mechanical experiments, including cadaveric repair testing, check the models’ assumptions and predictions. We are investigating how fixation, scaffold augmentation, and implant design affect stability, with the aim of informing surgical planning and identifying designs that merit clinical evaluation.

Gluteus Medius Pathology

Gluteus medius and minimus tears are often missed in patients with lateral hip pain, weakness, or a persistent limp. We study how clinical presentation, MRI findings, and muscle degeneration relate to treatment and functional recovery. Clinical cohorts and evidence reviews examine repair techniques and the measures used to judge success, including pain, walking, and abductor function. This work is intended to clarify patient selection for repair and improve the information available when counseling patients about recovery.

Evidence at Population Scale

Treatment outcomes and complications can differ across patient groups in ways that small clinical series do not capture. We use large clinical datasets to study medication exposures, disease associations, implant survival, and outcomes after surgery. Analyses address differences between treated groups, incomplete follow-up, and other sources of bias in routine records. We also examine whether published prediction models perform in independent datasets. These studies are intended to support risk assessment and treatment decisions while keeping associations distinct from evidence of causation.
